Every behavioral interview series has to start here, because almost every real interview does. "Tell me about yourself" (or "walk me through your resume") is usually the opener, and engineers consistently underrate it because it feels informal, almost like small talk. It isn't. It's the moment the interviewer forms their first hypothesis about you, and first impressions anchor how they interpret everything you say afterward.

## What the interviewer is actually listening for

This question has no STAR structure because it isn't a single story — it's a **narrative**. The interviewer wants to know:

- Can you communicate clearly and concisely, without prompting?
- Is there a coherent thread to your career, or does it sound like a random walk?
- Do you know what you want, and does it plausibly line up with this role?

What they are *not* asking for is your full resume read aloud. If you're narrating "and then in 2021 I moved to team B where I worked on X, Y, and Z," you've already lost the room.

## A structure that works: Present → Past → Future

A reliable shape, usually 60–90 seconds long:

**1. Present (where you are now).**
Start with your current role and, briefly, the kind of work you do. One sentence.

> "I'm currently a backend engineer at [Company], where I work on the payments infrastructure team."

**2. Past (the throughline that got you here).**
Not a full history — the 2–3 beats that explain your trajectory and *why* you made the moves you made. This is where you show intentionality.

> "I started out doing full-stack work at a small startup, which is where I first got pulled into performance and scaling problems — our checkout flow was falling over under load, and fixing that got me hooked on backend and infra work. That pulled me toward my current role, where I've spent the last two years specializing in distributed systems."

**3. Future (why you're here, in this room).**
Connect your trajectory to the role you're interviewing for. This is the line that makes the whole narrative land.

> "I'm looking for a role where I can go deeper on distributed systems at larger scale, which is what drew me to this team."

That's it. Three beats, no resume recitation, and it ends by handing the interviewer a natural thread to pull on ("tell me more about the checkout flow problem" is a great place for them to go next — and notice, that's a question you've essentially engineered).

## Common mistakes

- **Going too long.** Two minutes is the ceiling. Beyond that, you're eating into interview time and the interviewer starts skimming rather than listening.
- **Listing companies instead of telling a story.** "I worked at A, then B, then C" has no throughline. Always answer "why" you moved, not just "where."
- **Ending without a connection to the role.** If your answer could be given verbatim to any interviewer at any company, it's too generic. Tailor the "future" beat to what this specific role or team actually does.
- **Undersharing personality.** You don't need a personal life story, but a single honest detail about what you actually enjoy about the work ("I got hooked on...") makes you memorable versus the tenth generic answer of the day.
- **Overpreparing to the point of sounding robotic.** Practice the structure and key beats, not a word-for-word script. It should sound like you're explaining your own career, not reciting a monologue.

## How to prepare this in advance

Unlike most behavioral questions, you should have this one essentially memorized in structure (not word-for-word) before any interview, because it comes up almost every time:

1. Write out your Present/Past/Future in bullet form.
2. Time yourself saying it out loud. If you're over 90 seconds, cut detail from the "Past" section first.
3. Adjust the "Future" beat per company — this is the one part that should change interview to interview.
4. Practice out loud, not just in your head. The gap between "I know what I want to say" and "I can say it fluently under mild stress" is bigger than people expect.

Get this one right and you set a strong tone for everything that follows. Get it wrong — rambling, unfocused, no clear direction — and you're playing catch-up for the rest of the interview.
